Logic games for kids: patterns, sequences and thinking step by step

By Lulu Kids · Published 2026-11-22 · Updated 2026-11-22

"Logical thinking" sounds serious, but in childhood it boils down to very everyday questions: what comes next? what does not belong in this group? what happens if I do this? Children explore them naturally while playing. This guide suggests simple ways to support that reasoning with patterns, sorting and sequences, from age three to eight.

What logical thinking is in a child

In practice, it includes recognizing patterns, comparing and sorting, ordering items in a sequence, deducing a rule and anticipating simple consequences. It does not require school knowledge: a three-year-old who organizes their cars by color is already sorting. What changes with age is the complexity of the rules they can handle.

Patterns: the first step

A pattern is a rule that repeats: red, blue, red, blue... Small children enjoy finding out what comes next. You can start with real objects: alternate stones and leaves, or clap and tap the table in rhythm. Then ask them to continue: "red, blue, red, what now?". Once they master AB patterns, try AAB (red, red, blue), ABC or growing patterns (one, two, three blocks).

Patterns show up everywhere: floor tiles, the stripes on a shirt, music, the days of the week. Pointing them out in daily life is a good way for them to get used to looking for them.

Sorting and grouping

Sorting means deciding which things go together and why. Give them a box of mixed objects (buttons, lids, small toys) and ask them to separate them however they like. Then ask: "how did you group them?". They may answer by color, size or shape. Then propose a new criterion: "can you separate them another way?". That flexibility, changing the rule, is an important step.

A classic game is "odd one out": show four objects, three of which share something (for example, three fruits and a shoe) and ask which does not belong and why. What matters is not only the answer but the justification.

Sequences and order

Ordering a series by size, or putting a story's pictures in order, works on understanding before and after. Try three or four cards showing an action (a seed, a small plant, a plant with a flower) and ask them to put them in order. Ask what happened first, and what next. Daily routines are also sequences: first we wash our hands, then we eat, then we tidy up.

Cause and effect

For small children, the world is a laboratory. Does it float or sink? What happens if I drop this? If you let them experiment with safe objects in the bath or kitchen, and ask "what do you think will happen?", you are encouraging hypotheses. They do not need to be right; what is valuable is that they think first and compare afterwards with what happened.

Simple board and card games

Board games with simple rules (dominoes, matching pairs, ladders) teach taking turns, planning a move and accepting rules. From five or six, games like tic-tac-toe or checkers introduce anticipating the other player's moves. If they lose, try to keep a relaxed attitude so that losing is not a drama (see more in the guide on handling frustration).

Logic on screen

A digital logic game can be a good complement if it offers gradual challenges, allows experimenting without penalty and explains with voice or images what is expected. It is better to avoid those that pressure with time, points or lives. In Lulu Kids, the logic block includes patterns, series and sorting with four levels, and a gentle mark shows the answer if the child makes a mistake so they can try again. As always, a few minutes is enough and it is best to join them when possible.

How to help without giving the answer

When they get stuck, avoid stating the solution outright. Try guiding questions: "what happens each time?", "what repeats?", "what changes?". If they get it wrong, do not say "no": say "shall we check?". Verifying their own answer teaches them to evaluate instead of depending on your approval.

What to expect by age

  • Age 3: sorts by one criterion (color or size) and continues simple patterns.
  • Ages 4 to 5: patterns with three elements, finds the odd one out, orders sequences of three or four steps.
  • Ages 6 to 7: combined rules (red and big), simple number series, basic strategy games.
  • Age 8 or older: riddles, deduction, planning several steps ahead.

These are general references: some children are more drawn to logic and others to music or movement, and all of it is valid.

A practical close

You can start today with something you already have: a row of cutlery to sort, a pasta necklace to make a pattern, or a simple question on a walk: "what repeats on that fence?". What matters is the dialogue, that the child explains how they think and feels their ideas count. For something more hands-on, puzzles are a great ally.
