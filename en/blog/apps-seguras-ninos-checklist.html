Safe apps for kids: a checklist for parents

By Lulu Kids · Published 2026-11-22 · Updated 2026-11-26

Installing an app for a child seems like a one-minute job, but behind a colorful screen there may be aggressive ads, accidental purchases, unnecessary permissions or data collection. You do not need to be a tech expert to protect them: a few checks before and after installing are enough. This guide gathers a practical checklist you can follow in a few minutes.

Before installing

  • Read the store listing. Review the description, the age rating, the developer and the data safety section, where the store shows what information the app says it collects.
  • Look for a privacy policy. A serious kids' app has a clear, accessible policy. If there is none, be wary.
  • Read reviews with judgment. Pay attention to repeated complaints about ads, charges or crashes, and be wary of overly generic reviews.
  • Check who makes it. A developer with other apps and a real contact site is a good sign.
  • Download only from official stores. Avoid installer files from unknown sites.

Permissions: less is better

A game for small children rarely needs access to your contacts, location, microphone or photos. If it asks, ask why. On Android you can review and revoke permissions in Settings. An app that works well while asking for almost nothing is usually a good sign. Lulu Kids, for example, asks for no name, email, location, photos, contacts or microphone, as detailed in its privacy policy.

Ads

Ads are one of the main sources of problems in kids' apps. Pay attention to:

  • Frequency. Do they appear every few seconds or only between games?
  • Type of content. Are they suitable for their age? Some networks let you set child-directed treatment and filter categories.
  • Closing. Is the close button clear, or hidden to provoke accidental taps?
  • Deceptive ads. Ones that imitate game buttons are bad practice.
  • Ad-free option. A reasonable one-time payment beats a confusing subscription.

If an app has a free version with ads, try it yourself for a few minutes to see how it feels.

In-app purchases

Many children make purchases by accident. To avoid it:

  • Turn on authentication (password, fingerprint or PIN) for every purchase in your device's store.
  • Disable purchases on the child's profile if the system allows.
  • Be wary of apps with coins, loot boxes or pressure to buy.
  • Occasionally review purchase history and active subscriptions.

A good practice is for purchases to sit behind an adult check, such as a question a small child cannot solve. Lulu Kids has no purchases and puts its Info screen and external links behind a multiplication question.

External links and social media

Check whether the app has links that lead out of the game (to a website, social media, other apps). Ideally they sit behind an adult check. It is also best to avoid apps with open chat or features for sharing content or location with strangers, which are not suitable for small children.

Data and privacy

Ask yourself: does the app need an account? Why? Educational games for small children can work without sign-up. If it asks for the child's name, exact age or photos, think twice. Read in the privacy policy what data is collected, who it is shared with and how it can be deleted. If the policy is vague or missing, that is reason enough not to install.

Device parental controls

  • Child profiles or kids mode. Android, iOS and tablets offer profiles with restrictions.
  • Time limits. You can set schedules or maximum times per app.
  • Content filters. Restrict the age rating in the store.
  • Download approval. Require your permission for every new app.
  • Airplane or offline mode. If the app works without internet, put the device in airplane mode while they play. It avoids ads that load from the network and reduces the risk of links. See more on playing offline.

Use the app yourself first

Before handing over the device, play for ten minutes yourself. Try the menu, the buttons, the settings. Look for what might be confusing or frustrating to a child: timers, error sounds, "you lost" messages, characters that cry when you fail. A good kids' game tries to make the child feel capable, not guilty.

Quick list

  • Has a clear privacy policy.
  • Asks for no account or unnecessary personal data.
  • Minimal permissions.
  • Few, appropriate and easily closed ads, or an ad-free option.
  • Purchases and external links behind a parental check.
  • No chat with strangers.
  • Works offline.
  • Uses no timers or pressure.
  • You tried it first.

Check from time to time

An app that is safe today can change with an update. Every so often, check the release notes, permissions and recent reviews. Talk with your child about what they do in the app and ask them to tell you if something makes them uncomfortable or scared. Knowing they can tell you without fear of losing the device is one of the best protections.

Closing

Choosing safe apps is a habit more than a chore: a few questions before installing and an occasional review are enough. If you also set a screen routine and choose games suited to their age (see how to choose educational games), the child can enjoy them with peace of mind.

Frequently asked questions

Which permissions should a game app for young children ask for?

It rarely needs access to your contacts, location, microphone or photos. If it asks, ask why. On Android you can review and remove permissions in Settings.

How do I avoid accidental purchases?

Turn on authentication (password, fingerprint or PIN) for every purchase in your device store, disable purchases in the child profile if the system allows it, be wary of apps with coins or surprise boxes, and check the purchase history and subscriptions from time to time.

Does a kids app need an account?

Educational games for young children can work without registration. If it asks for a name, exact age or photos of the child, think twice and read the privacy policy to see what data is collected.
