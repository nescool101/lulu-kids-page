A calm bedtime routine: how to make the evening easier for kids

By Lulu Kids · Published 2026-11-22 · Updated 2026-11-22

Every family has its own version of bedtime, and almost all of them agree on one thing: it can be the loveliest moment of the day or the most exhausting. The difference is rarely the child; it usually lies in whether there is a predictable routine that tells them, little by little, that the day is winding down. This guide suggests a simple night routine, explains why it helps and offers ideas for the days when nothing goes as planned.

Why a routine works

Young children do not handle time well yet. They do not grasp that "it is late" or that "tomorrow we have to get up early"; what they do understand is sequences: first this, then that, and finally bed. When the steps are always the same and in the same order, the child can anticipate what comes next and stops resisting every step as a surprise. A routine works like a map: it does not force, it guides. It also cuts down on negotiation, because the answer to "what if I don't want to?" is already in the order of the evening: this is how we always do it.

It does not have to be long or perfect. A routine of twenty to thirty minutes with three or four steps is usually enough. What matters is that you can keep it going on tired days, on days with visitors or when traveling. A routine that is only followed on good days does not do its job.

A five-step outline

You can adapt this outline to your home. Change the steps, the order or the length to whatever works with your child:

  • Advance notice. Ten minutes before, announce that the time is coming: "When this song ends, we start getting ready." A warning avoids the abrupt cut-off, which is what causes the most protests.
  • Put the day away. Tidy the toys together, switch off screens and dim the lights in the house a little. Letting the child take part, even by putting away a single stuffed animal, gives them a small task with a clear end.
  • Hygiene. Bath or wash, teeth, pajamas. Making it a fixed order helps: pajamas first, then teeth, then the story.
  • Story and conversation. Read together, talk about the best part of the day or sing a quiet song. It is the closest moment of the evening and it does not need to be long.
  • A fixed goodbye. A phrase, a hug or a gesture that is always the same: "Good night, see you tomorrow." The repeated gesture reassures because it marks the end.

Screens and bedtime

Many families wonder whether a screen right before bed is acceptable. General recommendations, like those covered in our guide on screen time, suggest avoiding it in the hour before bed, because excitement and light can make rest harder. If you want some play in the afternoon, put it before dinner or bath time, and leave the last stretch of the day for screen-free activities: stories, soft music, talking or a little drawing.

If your child usually asks for "one more game" before bed, you do not have to say no every night. It is enough for the rule to be clear and always the same: games end before the bath. When the rule does not change with the mood of the day, children stop testing it.

When the child gets up or asks for water

It is normal for a young child to ask for water, one more hug, a trip to the bathroom or to tell you something important just after being tucked in. Sometimes it is a real need, and sometimes it is a way to stretch out your company. It helps to anticipate: before turning off the light, ask whether they need water or the bathroom and offer a last hug. If they get up, walk them back to bed with few words and a calm voice, with no games or long conversations. Repeated patiently, the message is clear: night is for resting, and you are still close by.

If fear is what keeps them awake, do not dismiss it with a "nothing is going to happen." Acknowledge the feeling ("I understand the dark scares you") and offer something concrete: a dim light, a stuffed animal, leaving the door ajar. Stories about feelings can help name what they feel during the day, not only at night.

The days that do not go as planned

There will be nights with visitors, illness, time changes or vacations when the routine breaks down. That is fine. What helps is keeping the skeleton even when the length changes: even a ten-minute version, with the same order and the same goodbye. The next day, go back to the usual outline without drama. Children pick routines back up more easily than we tend to fear.

If you travel, bring a couple of items that are part of the routine: the usual story, the stuffed animal, the song. Familiar things help a new place feel safe. And if the trip is long, our guide to offline play can help you arrive with your child in a good mood.

Involving the child

Children accept rules better when they feel they have some control. Let them choose between two options that you are happy with: which pajamas to wear, which story to read, which stuffed animal will sleep with them. Avoid open questions such as "do you want to go to bed?", which will almost always get a no; it is better to ask "do you put on the blue pajamas first or the red ones?" That way the child feels they are deciding, and bedtime is still bedtime.

With slightly older children, you can make a small poster with drawings of each step and hang it in their room. Seeing it, ticking it off or touching it as each step is done gives a feeling of achievement. The idea is not to reward with gifts, but to let the order itself be the reward: finishing and being able to curl up calmly.

What to do if it does not work

If after a few weeks the routine is not helping, check a few points before changing everything. Are the steps always the same? Do screens end early enough? Did the child spend enough time moving and playing during the day? Is there a fear or worry we have not listened to? Sometimes it is enough to adjust the start time, because starting when the child is already too tired usually brings more tears, not fewer.

Every child is different, and persistent sleep problems deserve a conversation with their pediatrician. What we share here are general family-organization ideas, not medical advice.

One idea to start this week

Do not change everything at once. Pick a single step, for example the advance notice with a song, and keep it for seven days. When it is part of the evening, add the next one. With patience, in a few weeks you will have a routine that feels familiar to your child and gives you peace of mind. Bedtime does not have to be a battle: it can be the calm close of a shared day.
