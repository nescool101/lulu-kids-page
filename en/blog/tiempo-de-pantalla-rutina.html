Screen time for young kids: a calm routine

By Lulu Kids · Published 2026-11-22 · Updated 2026-11-26

Almost every family asks the same question: how much screen time is reasonable for a young child? There is no magic number that suits everyone, but a few simple principles help make screens a calm part of the day instead of a source of arguments. This guide suggests a practical, flexible routine that is easy to keep.

What the general guidelines say

In 2019 the World Health Organization published guidelines for children under five: for babies under one it recommends avoiding screens; for ages one to four it suggests no more than an hour a day, and less is better; and it always prioritizes active play, enough sleep and time with adults. These are general references, not individual medical rules: if you have doubts about your child, talk to their pediatrician. The key idea is that the screen is a complement, not the center of the day.

Quality matters as much as quantity

A video that plays endlessly is not the same as a short game with a clear goal. Slow-paced content with voice, little visual noise and no time pressure is usually kinder to a small child than something fast and overstimulating. Before letting them play, try it yourself for five minutes. Look for ads that interrupt, purchases triggered by a tap or links that lead outside the app. A good children's game should be usable with only occasional supervision.

A simple four-step routine

  1. Pick fixed moments. For example, a while after the afternoon snack or while you cook dinner. When the schedule is predictable, the child knows what to expect and there is less negotiating.
  2. Set the length before starting. Say: "we play until this song ends" or "two games and we stop." A limit is easier to accept when it is known from the start.
  3. Warn before ending. A "one more game" heads-up avoids the abrupt cut, which is what causes most meltdowns.
  4. Have the next activity ready. Drawing, going to the park, building with blocks or a snack. The transition is easier when something pleasant is waiting.

Times when it is better to skip screens

There are situations where a screen tends to get in the way. During meals, because conversation is lost and the child eats without noticing what they eat. In the hour before bed, because excitement and light can make rest harder. And as the only tool to calm a tantrum, because a child may link getting upset with receiving the device. Using it now and then on a long trip or in a waiting room is fine: the routine is what counts, not the exception.

Play with them, even briefly

The first few minutes spent with your child make the difference. Ask what they see, name the colors or animals, celebrate the right answers and laugh along. That way the screen becomes a conversation and not a babysitter. Over time, once they know the game, you can leave them for a while while you stay nearby. Afterwards, ask them to tell you what they did; it is a natural way to reinforce vocabulary and memory.

Handling negotiations

Children test limits, and that is normal. Keep rules simple and consistent: if today is two games, tomorrow is too. Avoid using the screen as a reward or punishment, because it gives it exaggerated value. If they get upset when it ends, acknowledge the feeling ("I know you wanted to keep going") without giving in, and offer the next activity. With consistency, most children adapt within a few weeks.

Balance with the rest of the day

A good check is whether your child has enough time to move, play outdoors, sleep, eat without distractions and talk with the family. If all that is covered, a short stretch of calm games fits in easily. If something is missing, it may be time to trim the screen a little and strengthen the rest.

Check the device settings

Turn on parental controls, disable purchases without a password and make sure the app does not ask for unnecessary permissions. Some children's apps, like Lulu Kids, put external links behind an adult check and ask for no account. Spending ten minutes on this review gives you peace of mind for the rest of the year.

There is no perfect family and no routine that works the same for everyone. Start small, watch how your child reacts and adjust. What matters most is that screen time is short, calm and shared, and that it leaves room for everything else a child needs to live.
