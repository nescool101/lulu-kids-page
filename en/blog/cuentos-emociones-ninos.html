Stories about feelings: how to talk with kids about what they feel

By Lulu Kids · Published 2026-11-22 · Updated 2026-11-22

A small child feels huge emotions and has very few words to describe them. When something bothers them, they may scream, cry or throw themselves on the floor, because they cannot yet say "I'm frustrated" or "I feel jealous". Stories are a valuable tool to help: they show emotions in characters, name what goes on inside and let you talk about it without the child feeling singled out. This guide explains how to make the most of them.

Why stories work

When a child hears that the little bear in the story is angry because his toy broke, they learn two things: that the emotion has a name and that others feel it too. Talking about the character creates a comfortable distance; it is easier to ask "why do you think the bear shouted?" than "why did you shout?". Over time, that emotional vocabulary moves into their life: "I feel like the bear".

What to look for in a story

  • Clear emotions. Characters whose faces and actions show what they feel.
  • Familiar situations. Sharing toys, losing something, the first day of school, a new sibling.
  • A kind resolution. Everything need not end perfectly, but the character should find a healthy way to deal with what they feel.
  • Simple language and rhythm. Rhymes and repetition help the youngest follow the story.
  • No forced morals. If the story lectures, the child tunes out.

The basic emotions

To begin, four or five are enough: joy, sadness, anger, fear and, later, surprise or shame. You can name them in the story and in daily life: "I see you're sad", "it looks like you got angry". Naming does not mean approving any behavior: you can accept the emotion and set limits on the action ("I understand you're angry, but we don't hit").

How to read it, step by step

  • Look at the cover and predict. "How do you think this rabbit feels?"
  • Pause on the illustrations. Faces say a lot; let the child describe them.
  • Ask open questions. "What do you think he'll do now?" "What would help him?"
  • Connect to their life without pressure. "Do you remember when you felt like that?" If they do not want to answer, do not insist.
  • Reread favorites. Repetition helps the child absorb the message.

Beyond the book

Not every story lives in a book. You can invent stories with toys: a bear who is afraid of the dark, a dinosaur who does not want to share. Puppets or drawings work too. A useful game: the child picks a stuffed animal that feels something and asks you to help: "the bear is sad, what do we do?". By helping the toy, they rehearse strategies they can use themselves (hugging, breathing, asking for help, counting to five).

Tools for calming down

Stories can teach self-regulation strategies: breathing as if smelling a flower and blowing out a candle, squeezing and releasing a cushion, going to a "calm corner", drawing what we feel. Try these in a quiet moment, not when the child is in the middle of a tantrum, because it is hard to learn something new then. With practice, they can turn to them when needed. Drawing and painting is a very good way to express emotions without words.

For older children

From five or six, you can talk about more complex emotions: envy, shame, disappointment. Stories with dilemmas or characters who make mistakes let you ask: "what did they do well? what could they do differently?". You can also ask them to write or draw an alternative ending. At this age they may read alone; keep reading aloud together anyway, because the conversation afterwards is still what matters.

Stories on screen

There are digital stories with voice, illustrations and small interactions. They can be a useful support when no book is at hand, as long as they are calm, free of intrusive ads and paced to allow conversation. Lulu Kids has voiced stories about feelings, meant to be listened to and discussed with an adult. Even so, a book on the lap, with conversation and contact, has a value a screen does not replace, so it helps to combine both in a balanced way (see the guide on the screen-time routine).

Common mistakes

  • Saying "don't cry" or "it's not a big deal". It minimizes the emotion; "I understand you're sad" is better.
  • Interrupting the story with many questions. Let the story flow and comment at key moments.
  • Using the story as punishment or a lecture. It should be a moment of closeness, not reproach.
  • Expecting immediate answers. Sometimes a child processes the conversation hours or days later.

When to ask for help

Intense emotions are normal in childhood. If you notice very persistent sadness or fear, strong changes in sleep or appetite, very frequent tantrums that are hard to manage, or difficulty relating to others lasting weeks, it is worth talking to the pediatrician or a child psychologist. Asking for help is not failure; it is part of caring.

Closing

A bedtime story, ten minutes of calm conversation, a hug: nothing more is needed to start. By naming emotions with your child, you give them a gift for life: knowing what they feel and that someone listens. To continue with frustrations, read how to handle frustration in games.
