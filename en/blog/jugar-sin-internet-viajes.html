Offline play: how to survive trips, waits and waiting rooms with kids

By Lulu Kids · Published 2026-11-22 · Updated 2026-11-22

Every parent knows the moment: a waiting room, a long trip, a restaurant with slow service, and a child starting to get bored. The temptation is to pull out the phone, but the signal fails, the battery drains and sometimes what appears on screen is not what we would like. Having a repertoire of games that work without internet, with and without screens, is one of the most useful parenting tools. This guide offers ideas for each situation.

Why it helps not to depend on internet

  • No connection when you most need it. Planes, tunnels, rural areas and hospital basements are often without signal.
  • Fewer distractions and risks. Without internet there are no ads loading, no links opened by mistake and no unexpected content.
  • Saves data and battery. An offline game uses less.
  • More calm. The child plays without notifications or interruptions.

That is why it is a good idea that the apps you install work offline. If you are not sure, test in airplane mode before leaving.

Before leaving: the activity bag

Prepare a small bag or backpack with things not used at home every day, so they have some novelty:

  • A notebook and pencils or crayons.
  • Reusable stickers.
  • A small pad of stories or a couple of short books.
  • Cards or a small matching game.
  • A small figure or stuffed toy.
  • A water bottle and a safe snack (eating is entertainment too).
  • A device with downloaded games and headphones if there are other passengers.

Saving a few things "for the trip" and not handing them out earlier increases interest.

In the car

  • I spy. "I spy something red." The classic that never fails and adapts to any age.
  • The word game. One person says a word; the next says another starting with the last letter or syllable.
  • Count and find. Count how many red cars, bridges or cows you see on a stretch.
  • Chained stories. Each person adds a sentence to a story. Very fun and quick to start.
  • Songs. The ones repeated again and again are a lifesaver.
  • Audio stories. Download stories or music beforehand to listen to without a screen.

For children who get carsick from reading or screens, prioritize spoken games.

On a plane or train

Besides the above, offer small-table activities: activity books, stickers, drawing, play dough (in sealed bags). A downloaded screen game can save a tricky moment, and it is an excellent occasion for a calm one without loud sounds (use headphones with a volume limit). Remember to plan movement breaks: walking the aisle, stretching, going to the restroom.

In the waiting room

Medical waits can be unsettling for a child. Quiet games like "What's missing?" or card matching are ideal (there are ideas in the guide on memory games). If the child is nervous, a story about the doctor or a drawing can help. Speak softly about what will happen so they know what to expect: anticipation reduces anxiety.

In a restaurant

Bring a small kit: a couple of crayons, sheets, stickers. Games with napkins, cups and cutlery (carefully) or sugar packets can fill a few minutes. A silent digital game can be a last resort: lower the volume and let them use it for a few minutes while the food arrives, then return to table conversation. Avoid making it the norm if you want them to learn to wait and to talk.

Offline digital games: what to look for

If you decide to use a screen, choose a game that:

  • Truly works offline after downloading.
  • Has no timers or pressure.
  • Has few or no ads. Lulu Kids works without internet, collects no personal data and offers an ad-free version for a one-time payment.
  • Has sound under control: no unexpected loud noises.
  • Is downloaded and tested before leaving.

There are more criteria in the guide Safe apps for kids.

How to avoid it always being the screen

A common trap is using the screen as the immediate fix every time there is boredom. A bit of boredom is healthy: it lets the child invent. Try this sequence: first a spoken game, then a hands-on activity, and the screen as a last resort. And remember the time limit is easier to hold if announced beforehand (see the screen-time routine).

On returning

A long trip or a tricky wait can leave the child with pent-up energy. Plan a few minutes of free movement on arrival: running, jumping, stretching. And if there was more screen than usual, that is fine: return to the normal routine the next day with no guilt.

Closing

With a small bag, a few spoken ideas and a couple of downloaded games, almost any wait becomes manageable. The most valuable part is that the trip or wait is shared time: a song sung together is worth more than any screen.
