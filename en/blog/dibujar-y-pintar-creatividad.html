Drawing and painting: how to nurture creativity without directing the child

By Lulu Kids · Published 2026-11-22 · Updated 2026-11-22

When a small child grabs a pencil or a brush, they are not trying to make a work of art: they are exploring. They move their arm, leave marks, discover that their actions produce visible results. That discovery is the beginning of creativity and also of writing. This guide explains what to expect from children's drawing, which materials to offer and how to accompany without directing.

The stages of drawing

Although every child is unique, drawing usually follows a recognizable path:

  • Free scribbling (ages 1 to 2). Big, random strokes born from arm movement. The gesture matters, not the result.
  • Controlled scribbling (ages 2 to 3). Lines, circles and dots appear and the child begins repeating them on purpose.
  • First shapes (ages 3 to 4). Closed circles emerge and, sometimes, the famous "tadpole": a head with legs and arms coming out of it.
  • Symbolic drawing (ages 4 to 6). Houses, trees, people with bodies and a sun in a corner appear. The child tells stories with what they draw.
  • More detail (ages 6 to 8). Backgrounds, proportions and complete scenes are added, and they become more self-critical.

These ages are rough. A child who at four only scribbles is not "behind": maybe they just like something else more.

Materials to offer

You do not need to spend much. What matters is that they are safe and varied.

  • Thick and wax crayons. Easy to grip with vivid colors.
  • Finger paint or washable tempera. Ideal for exploring with the hands.
  • Big paper. Wrapping paper, flattened cardboard or paper rolls allow broad strokes.
  • Thick brushes, sponges, rollers. Varying tools gives a variety of marks.
  • Things from nature. Leaves, sticks, stones to print or draw with.

Check that paints and materials are non-toxic and age-appropriate, and supervise under-threes so nothing goes into their mouths.

Process, not product

A key idea in children's art is that the process matters. If a child spends half an hour mixing all the colors until they get brown, they have learned something about color, even if the "painting" looks like a mess. Avoid asking them to draw something specific ("draw me a cat") or correcting them ("cats aren't green"). Let them decide. If they want a pink sky, it is a pink sky.

How to comment on their drawings

Saying "how pretty" to everything is kind but loses meaning over time. Try comments that describe and ask:

  • "I see you used a lot of blue. What is this?"
  • "Will you tell me what's happening here?"
  • "I like how you made these long lines."
  • "You worked hard on this, it shows."

Avoid guessing what it is ("is that a dog?"), because if you are wrong the child gets discouraged. Better: "tell me about your drawing". Sometimes they answer with a long story; other times, "it's a drawing". Both are fine.

Creating a space and a habit

A corner with a low table, an old apron and a box of materials within reach encourages drawing without asking permission every time. Covering the table with an oilcloth or newspaper reduces worry about stains. Keep their work in a folder or hang it with clothespins on a string: seeing their work displayed builds confidence.

Ideas to try

  • Drawing with eyes closed. Fun and surprising.
  • Painting to music. Change the rhythm and watch the strokes change.
  • Draw an emotion. What does anger look like, or joy? It connects with stories about feelings.
  • Team drawing. Each person does a part of the same sheet.
  • Floor mural. A long strip of paper and lots of colors.
  • Color mixing. A good companion to colors and shapes.

A good base for writing

Drawing strengthens the hand, eye-hand coordination and pencil control, skills used later for writing. It also widens vocabulary, because the child needs words to explain their ideas. There is no need to push them to "write" early: scribbles and drawings are already a way of communicating. To go further, see the guide on learning letters without pressure.

Painting on screen

A digital drawing game can be a good option on trips or when there is no room to make a mess: a color palette, brushes of several thicknesses, an eraser and the chance to save or start over. Lulu Kids includes a painting block with these ideas, with no timer or points. It does not replace paper, the texture of a pencil or the satisfaction of getting your hands dirty, but it is a practical, clean alternative. Remember that the first minutes are the most valuable and that it is best to alternate with other activities.

When not to worry

If your child does not want to draw, that is not a problem. Some children prefer building, dancing or singing. Offer opportunities without pressure and respect their interest. If you notice marked difficulty gripping objects, or that they always avoid any fine motor activity past four or five, mention it to the pediatrician to rule out coordination difficulties.

Closing

When a child draws, they are thinking, feeling and practicing. Offer materials, time and attention, and let creativity do the rest. Keep their drawings: in a few years they will be a treasure.
