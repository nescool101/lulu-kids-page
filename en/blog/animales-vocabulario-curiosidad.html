Animals for kids: vocabulary, curiosity and respect for nature

By Lulu Kids · Published 2026-11-22 · Updated 2026-11-22

Few topics attract young children as much as animals. A dog in the park, a snail in the garden, a lion in a story: all of them provoke questions, imitations and sounds. That natural curiosity is an excellent opportunity to learn vocabulary, develop observation and pass on respect for living things. This guide offers practical ideas to make use of it, from age two to eight.

Why animals work so well

Animals are concrete, visible, noisy and moving, which makes them an ideal learning object. They also awaken emotions: tenderness, fear, wonder. A child who feels something for an animal remembers better what they learn about it. They are also a topic that connects with almost everything: colors (a flamingo is pink), numbers (four legs), shapes, sounds, movements, food and habitat.

Vocabulary: from word to concept

The first animals a child learns are usually those in their surroundings and in stories: dog, cat, cow, duck. Over time, others are added: elephant, giraffe, dolphin. It helps a lot to repeat the name along with the sound ("the duck says quack") and the gesture, and to link it with a picture and, if possible, a real animal. As they grow, you can add categories: "a dog is a mammal, a parrot is a bird". They do not need to memorize scientific terms, but understanding that there are groups prepares orderly thinking.

If your family is bilingual, animals are perfect ground for practicing both languages: dog and perro, cat and gato, bird and pájaro. There is more on this in the guide on bilingual kids.

Imitation games

Imitating animals moves the body and trains memory. Hop like a rabbit, slither like a snake, walk like a bear, fly like a butterfly. Ask them to guess which animal you are, then swap roles. Sounds work the same way: "who says meow?". At the end of the game, encourage them to invent a new animal combining parts of several (with a cat's body and bird wings, for instance), which works on creativity.

Really observing

A walk in the park or garden can become a small expedition. Bring a magnifying glass, a notebook or just your eyes. Look for ants, snails, birds, butterflies. Ask open questions: "what do you think it's doing?", "where do you think it lives?", "what does it eat?". If you do not know the answer, say so and look it up together: learning that adults also investigate is valuable. Note or draw what you observed; drawing is an excellent way to fix details.

Caring and respecting

Animals are a good door into talking about empathy. Some ideas:

  • Show how to approach a pet calmly, without shouting or chasing.
  • Explain that wild animals are watched without touching or bothering them.
  • If you have a pet, let them take part in simple care (filling the water, brushing) under your supervision.
  • Talk about what each animal needs: food, water, a safe place.
  • Pick up litter on walks so "animals don't get hurt".

Always supervise contact with animals, especially with children under five, and teach handwashing afterwards. Do not force a child who is afraid of an animal: you can start by looking from a distance.

Stories and songs with animals

Books and songs with animals are an endless resource: repetitive, rhythmic and with endearing characters. Look for stories where animals feel emotions (fear, joy, anger), because they open conversations about feelings. If there is singing, even better: songs with animal sounds are a great way to practice phonetics. Find more ideas in the guide on stories about feelings.

Animals on screen

An animal game on screen can be a good complement if it shows clear illustrations or photographs, says the animal's name and sound, and allows repeating without penalty. Lulu Kids has an animals block that combines image, voice and sound, in Spanish and English, designed as a small entry point to be continued with books, walks and conversation. A real visit to the zoo, a farm or the park is never entirely replaced.

Learning letters with animals

Animals are also a great support for the alphabet: "A for ant, B for bear, C for cat". Pictures give meaning to the sound, and children often remember the letter better when it is tied to an animal they like. There are more ideas in the guide on learning letters without pressure.

Ideas for a week

  • Monday: go out to watch insects or birds in a park.
  • Wednesday: play at imitating animals and guessing.
  • Friday: read a story with animals and draw the favorite one.
  • Weekend: visit a farm, a zoo or an aquarium if possible.

Closing

If your child becomes obsessed with dinosaurs, sharks or insects for weeks, that is a very good sign: deep curiosity is a source of learning. Follow their interest, offer related books and outings, and enjoy learning alongside them.
