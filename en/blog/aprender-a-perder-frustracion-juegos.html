Learning to lose: how to help a child handle frustration in games

By Lulu Kids · Published 2026-11-22 · Updated 2026-11-22

A child who flips the board when they lose, cries because the puzzle did not work or says "I can't do it" and quits is a normal child. Learning to tolerate frustration is one of the most important tasks of childhood, and games are an ideal training ground, because the risk is low and the emotions are real. This guide explains why it is so hard and suggests practical ways to help.

Why losing is so hard

Small children do not yet have self-control developed. For a three- or four-year-old, losing can feel like personal failure: "I lost, I'm bad". Egocentric thinking is also common at that age: winning is normal, losing is unfair. Over the years, the child comes to understand that games have rules, that the result does not define their worth and that others deserve to win too. That maturing takes time and advances in leaps.

Before playing: setting the stage

  • Set the rules beforehand. "We'll play two rounds and then tidy up." With clear rules, there are fewer arguments.
  • Talk about someone winning and someone losing. "Today one of us may win, and that's okay." Saying it calmly normalizes it.
  • Choose games suited to their age. Pure luck games (like a dice race) are easier to accept because they do not depend on skill.
  • Cooperative games. There are games where everyone wins or loses together against the game. They are a good starting point.

In the moment: how to respond

When frustration arrives, your reaction matters more than any explanation:

  • Stay calm. Your tone is contagious. Breathe.
  • Acknowledge the emotion. "You're angry because you wanted to win." Naming it helps process it (more in stories about feelings).
  • Set limits on behavior, not on emotion. "You can be angry, but we don't throw the pieces."
  • Offer a way out. A hug, a glass of water, a two-minute break.
  • Do not lecture in the heat of it. Reflection comes later, once they have calmed down.
  • Do not always let them win. Letting a child always win deprives them of learning to lose. Alternate.

Afterwards: reflecting without pressure

Once calm, ask simple questions: "What was the most fun?", "What would you do differently?". Avoid judging. You can share a time you lost and how you felt. When children see adults also make mistakes and handle it with humor, they learn naturally.

Useful phrases

  • "Losing is hard, I know. It was a good game."
  • "I liked how you tried."
  • "We all make mistakes; that's how we learn."
  • "It's not working yet. You can keep practicing."
  • "Do you want a rematch or would you rather rest?"

Notice the "yet": it communicates that skill grows with practice, and it is a way of speaking that encourages perseverance.

Phrases best avoided

  • "Don't cry, it's just a game." It minimizes what they feel.
  • "Look at your brother, he knows how to lose." It compares and humiliates.
  • "If you keep this up we won't play anymore." A threat may be needed once in a while, but it should not be the usual answer.
  • "You're a sore loser." It labels the person instead of describing the behavior.

Mistakes and practice

For a small child, making a mistake in a game is a source of frustration, which is why it matters that games show them that error is part of the process. In Lulu Kids, for example, there are no scores or timers, and when the child gets it wrong no "wrong" message appears: a gentle mark shows the answer and the child tries again. It is designed precisely so the child practices without fear of failing. But what teaches most is your attitude: celebrating effort and not only results. To train patience with longer tasks, puzzles and logic games are good allies.

Growth mindset, without overdoing it

There is a lot of talk about "growth mindset", the idea that skills develop with effort. Praising the process ("you worked hard") instead of ability ("you're so smart") is a sensible practice. It need not become a magic formula: no study promises that a few phrases alone will change a child. But the child feeling that their mistakes do not disappoint you does matter.

Perfectionists

Some children get frustrated not so much by losing as by wanting everything to be perfect. If your child erases their drawing ten times or quits a game at the first mistake, help them name it: "I see you want it to turn out perfect". Show your own smudges and how you fix them. Celebrate what went well within the imperfect.

What to expect by age

  • Ages 2 to 3: losing does not yet make sense; prefer games without a winner.
  • Ages 4 to 5: starts to grasp turns and rules; tantrums on losing are common.
  • Ages 6 to 7: accepts rules better but may be very competitive.
  • Age 8 or older: understands sportsmanship; can discuss strategies and results.

When to consult

If the frustration is very intense, frequent, with self-harm or aggression, or interferes with school and family life for weeks, talk to the pediatrician or a child psychologist. Most of the time it is a normal part of development, but it is better to ask.

Closing

Learning to lose is a slow process. Every game is a chance to practice with affection. If you manage to make your child feel they can make mistakes and still be loved, you have achieved the most important thing.
