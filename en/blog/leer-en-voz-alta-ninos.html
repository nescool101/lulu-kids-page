Reading aloud to kids: how to choose books and enjoy them

By Lulu Kids · Published 2026-11-22 · Updated 2026-11-22

Reading aloud to a child is one of the simplest and most loved habits a family can have. It needs no special materials, fits into any moment of the day and works just as well with a baby as with an eight-year-old. This guide offers practical ideas for reading aloud with children aged 2 to 8: how to choose books, how to read them with feeling even if you are no actor, what questions to ask and what to do when your child will not sit still.

Why reading aloud is worth it

When you read to your child, you offer vocabulary they may not hear in everyday conversation, you show them how a well-built sentence sounds and you teach them that books are a pleasant place. Above all, you give them your attention. That stretch of closeness, with a warm body beside you and a shared story, becomes an important memory over the years. Reading aloud does not replace other ways of learning, such as playing or talking, but it combines well with them. How to choose a book

There is no perfect book for every age, but these general ideas help:

  • At age 2, books with big pictures, few words per page, rhymes and repetition tend to work. At this age they like to ask for the same story many times, and that is normal: repetition gives them security.
  • At age 4, stories with a small plot appear: a problem, a character who tries and an ending. Books with animals, family routines and easy-to-recognize emotions are good.
  • At age 6, they can follow longer stories with short chapters, and they begin to enjoy humor, riddles and books of curious facts.
  • At age 8 and up, many children enjoy chapter novels read a little each night, even when they already read on their own. Listening to a story harder than they could read themselves is a gift.

Let the child take part in the choice. Visiting a library or a bookshop, or simply looking at the shelf at home, and choosing between two or three options gives them a leading role. If they always ask for the same book, you can occasionally offer a new one next to the favorite, without forcing it.

How to read with feeling even if you are no actor

You do not need perfect voices. Some simple tricks:

  • Read slowly. Children need time to imagine what they hear and to look at the pictures.
  • Change your tone a little between characters, or your speed in tense moments. That is enough.
  • Pause. Stopping before you turn the page and asking "what do you think will happen?" keeps curiosity alive.
  • Point at the pictures and name what appears, especially with the youngest. This widens vocabulary.
  • If you slip up or get tired, it is fine. What the child remembers is that you were with them.

Questions that invite conversation

It is less about quizzing and more about chatting. Some open questions that work with almost any story:

  • Which part did you like most? Why?
  • How do you think this character feels?
  • What would you do in their place?
  • What do you think will happen next?
  • Does it remind you of something you lived?

You do not have to ask every question or in every book. If the child just wants to listen and look, respect that. Questions work best when they come from shared enjoyment, not from duty. When the child will not stay still

Many young children cannot stay still for more than a few minutes, and that is fine. Let them move while they listen: they can draw, play with a toy or sit on your lap and then get up. If they lose interest, shorten the session, and if you see they are enjoying it, stretch it out. What matters is that story time is pleasant, not a task with a minimum length. A trick that sometimes works is reading in a special place, such as a blanket on the floor or the parents' bed, so that the place is linked with calm.

Reading in two languages

If your family speaks Spanish and English, you can alternate books in each language, or read the same story in both on different days. An idea many families use is to assign one language to each adult or to each time of day. You will find more suggestions in the guide on bilingual kids in Spanish and English. There is no need to translate line by line; it is natural to enjoy the story in the language chosen for that moment and talk about it afterwards in whichever you prefer.

Books and screens

Voiced stories in an app can be a good complement when you cannot read at that moment, for example during a trip, but they do not carry the same value as contact with you. When the child can already read

Even if your child reads on their own, keep reading aloud if they enjoy it. You can take turns: a page for you, a page for them. Or you read the long chapters and let them read the short ones. Listening is a way to approach more complex texts, and sharing a favorite book creates conversations that would not happen otherwise. Do not turn reading into a test: do not correct every mispronounced word or quiz them like an exam. If the child stumbles, give them the word naturally and keep going.

A routine that is easy to keep

Choose a time of day when reading is realistic, for example after the bath or after the afternoon snack, and start with ten minutes. If one day it is not possible, read just one page; keeping the habit matters more than the length. Over time, your child will ask for "another story" on their own, and that request will be the best sign that reading is already part of their day.
