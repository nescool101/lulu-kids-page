First numbers: how to teach counting through play

By Lulu Kids · Published 2026-11-22 · Updated 2026-11-22

Many children recite "one, two, three, four, five" at two and a half, and adults get excited. It is a good start, but reciting the number sequence is like singing a song: it does not yet mean the child understands what quantity each number stands for. This guide explains the difference and suggests simple games to help counting make sense, with no pressure or worksheets.

Reciting is not counting

Real counting involves several things at once. The child must know the sequence of words in order, assign one word to each object without skipping or repeating any, and understand that the last word said tells how many there are in total. That last idea is often called "cardinality" and it takes the longest to arrive. If you ask "how many cookies are there?" and the child counts again from one instead of answering "four", they are still building that concept. That is normal and does not need forcing.

Signs they are learning

  • They touch or point to each object while saying a number (one-to-one correspondence).
  • Sometimes they skip an object or count the same one twice; this improves with practice.
  • They can hand you "two" things when asked, even if they cannot yet count well beyond three.
  • They compare quantities with words like "more" or "less", even without knowing the exact number.

Everyday games that work

  • Stairs and steps. Count the stairs aloud as you climb and the steps to the door. Movement helps keep a steady rhythm.
  • Snack time. "Can you give me three grapes?" Putting out the requested amount is much more useful than repeating numbers with no objects.
  • Setting the table. One plate per person is a natural way to practice one-to-one correspondence.
  • Songs and finger rhymes. Number songs (five little ducks, ten little fingers) reinforce the sequence. Add the gesture of showing the matching fingers.
  • Simple cards or dice. With a die, the child rolls, counts the dots and moves that many spaces along a drawn path.

Recognizing quantities without counting

Before counting, children can perceive very small quantities at a glance: one, two, sometimes three. You can use this with "how many do you see?" games using counters, lids or chickpeas. Show two counters for one second and cover them: "how many were there?". Over time, the child starts to see groupings (a die showing five, the fingers of one hand), and that will be the base for adding later.

From 1 to 10, and then to 20

There is no need to jump quickly to big numbers. A child who counts confidently to ten, understanding that "ten" means ten objects, is in a better position than one who recites to fifty with no meaning. Once ten is solid, you can extend counting to twenty and start introducing ideas like "one more" and "one less": "you have four blocks, if I give you one more, how many do you have?".

The numbers 11 to 15 are hard in English and Spanish because they do not follow an obvious pattern (eleven, twelve, thirteen... / once, doce, trece...). If they get mixed up there, it is a known difficulty, not a fault in your child.

Where a screen game helps

A good number game on screen offers objects the child can touch and count, a voice that says each number as it is touched, and the chance to try again without penalty. Avoid games with countdowns, points or competition, which add stress to something that should be calm. In Lulu Kids, the numbers block is designed along these lines, with four levels (2, 4, 6 and 8+) so the child picks the one they find comfortable. And remember: five or ten minutes is enough; the rest of the learning happens off the screen.

How it changes by age

  • Age 2: counting up to two or three objects, understanding "one" and "many", singing number songs.
  • Age 3: counting to five or ten with support, putting out "three" things when asked.
  • Age 4: counting to ten with correspondence, recognizing a few written numerals (1, 2, 3).
  • Ages 5 to 6: counting to twenty, comparing quantities, starting small additions and subtractions with objects.
  • Age 7 or older: addition and subtraction with bigger numbers, discovering patterns and starting multiplication tables with the help of games.

These are general references, not required goals: every child moves at their own pace.

What to do if they get stuck

If your child gets frustrated, lower the difficulty: fewer objects, bigger or more spread-out objects, or return to a game they have mastered. Taking a break and resuming the next day usually works better than pushing. Avoid saying "don't you know four?"; prefer "let's count it together". If after six or seven there is a persistent and obvious difficulty with numbers, mention it to the teacher or pediatrician to explore support.

A practical close

Pick one activity from this list today and do it casually: counting the stairs, setting the table, sharing out grapes. Repeat several times during the week. The most valuable part is not the list of games but that the child sees numbers are useful for something real. Once they are comfortable with quantities, memory games and puzzles are a good next step.
